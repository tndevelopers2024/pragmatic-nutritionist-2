Frequently asked questions
01
What should I eat before a workout or game?
Focus on easily digestible carbs for energy (e.g., bananas, oatmeal, or whole-grain bread) and a small amount of protein to support muscles. Eat 2-3 hours before exercise.
02
What’s the best post-workout recovery meal?
A combination of protein (for muscle repair) and carbohydrates (to replenish glycogen) is ideal—examples include a protein shake with fruit or chicken with rice and vegetables.
03
How much protein do athletes need?
Protein requirements vary by sport and training intensity but generally range from 1.2–2.0 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight daily.
04
How important is meal timing for athletes?
Timing meals and snacks around workouts is crucial. Eating carbs and protein before and after exercise can optimize energy, recovery, and muscle repair.
05
How do I stay hydrated during sports?
Drink water regularly and consume electrolytes during prolonged activities (over an hour) to replace lost sodium, potassium, and magnesium.

09
What does a DNA-informed sports nutrition plan include?
Most athletes follow nutrition advice built for a statistical average. DNA-informed sports nutrition replaces that average with your actual biology.
A genetic assessment looks at the markers most relevant to athletic performance, how your body oxidises fat versus carbohydrate during exercise, your muscle fibre composition and recovery profile, your inflammatory and injury risk, your power and endurance genetic predispositions, and how efficiently you absorb and use nutrients like iron, Vitamin D, B12, and caffeine.
This data is then layered into your performance nutrition plan. So rather than following a standard high-carbohydrate endurance protocol because that is what most plans recommend, your plan is built around whether your genetics support that approach, or whether a different macro distribution would serve your body and your sport better. It is the difference between a plan that is generally correct and one that is correct for you.
10
How is sports nutrition different for elite athletes versus recreational athletes?
Recreational athletes benefit from general healthy eating principles. Elite athletes require periodised nutrition, meaning intake, macro ratios, and timing shift deliberately across training blocks, taper weeks, competition days, and recovery phases. The margin for error is smaller, the stakes are higher, and the plan needs to hold up across travel, tournaments, and high training loads.
11
Do you provide nutrition support for Ironman and triathlon training?
Yes. We build periodised plans for long-course triathlon covering fueling across swim-bike-run training blocks, brick-session nutrition, carb loading, race-day fueling strategy, and heat-adjusted hydration, so your nutrition scales with training volume and holds up on race day.
